Digital Inequality Is Social Inequality

February 17, 2025

In their article “Digital Inequalities and Why They Matter,” Robinson and others (2015) explain that digital inequality, like not having internet or tech skills, is not separate from other social problems like racism, sexism, or poverty. Instead, it’s closely connected to them. They argue that digital inequality should be taken just as seriously as issues related to race, gender, or class. After reading this, I started to see the internet differently, not just as a neutral tool, but as a space that reflects who has power and access.

What stood out to me most was the difference between first-level and second-level digital divides. The first level is about having access to devices and the internet. The second level is about having the skills and confidence to use them well. Many people think digital inequality only means not having internet or a computer, but the article shows that even people who are online might not have the knowledge to use it in helpful ways. This reminded me of growing up in a poor community where internet cafes were our only way to get online. We mostly used it for fun, not for schoolwork, job hunting, or building careers. I didn’t even know about things like LinkedIn or online portfolios until much later. Looking back, it wasn’t just a tech issue, it was a lost chance to grow and succeed.

It says that having access to the internet doesn’t automatically create fairness, it can actually make unfairness worse. For example, women might be online but feel unsure about using digital tools because of gender stereotypes. People of color might use the internet but still be left out of tech jobs or leadership roles. I see this happening here, where some township schools have smartboards or tablets, but no one knows how to use them well because there are no trained teachers or there are power cuts.

Another important part of the article is how it looks at digital inequality across a person’s life. It shows that digital challenges can affect people from childhood all the way to old age. That made me think of my grandmother. She can read and write, but now that many health services are online, she finds them hard to use. She feels left out, confused, and has to rely on others. As a result, her health sometimes suffers.

I also liked how the article talks about digital research. As a digital arts student ,I do web-based projects. The article made me think: Am I unintentionally leaving out people who don’t use the internet much or don’t feel comfortable online? It’s easy to assume that everyone uses digital tools like I do, but that’s not true, and Robinson et al. remind us that online data doesn’t always show the full picture of society.

In the end, this article isn’t just pointing out problems, it’s asking us to do something about them. It pushes us to include digital inequality in all areas of research and policy. For me, it brings up important ethical questions: How can I use my skills in a way that helps people instead of leaving some behind? How can I design things that include everyone, not just people like me?

Today, when so many parts of life, like school, healthcare, work, and even protests, happen online, not being digitally connected isn’t just a small problem. It’s a serious form of exclusion. As someone who wants to create digital media that teaches and empowers, I now realize that fighting digital inequality is not a side issue, it’s central to achieving fairness and justice.
