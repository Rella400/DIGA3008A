Essay 1: Evaluating Ethical UX Design on Superbalist.com in the South African Context

April 21, 2025

Superbalist.com is a well-known South African online store that sells fashion, beauty, and home items. Owned by Takealot Online (Pty) Ltd, it has gained popularity due to its easy-to- use interface, local content, and an overall shopping experience that aligns with South African consumer habits. As mobile technology plays a bigger role in how people interact with digital platforms, it is important to analyse Superbalist’s design not only from a usability perspective but from an ethical one as well. In today’s digital age, user experience (UX) goes beyond aesthetic appeal and smooth functionality; it also involves the moral implications of design decisions. Ethical UX design ensures that digital experiences respect users' rights, helping them make informed decisions without unnecessary pressure. This essay explores how Superbalist’s UX design affects users in terms of autonomy, fairness, inclusivity, and transparency, particularly within the South African context. By examining Superbalist's interface, interaction patterns, and design practices, this analysis seeks to evaluate how ethical or unethical the platform is. Through references to key theorists and frameworks such as Ghanchi (2021), Mathur et al. (2019), Costanza-Chock (2020), and Shoshana Zuboff (2019), the discussion will highlight how specific features of Superbalist’s design either empower or exploit users. The goal is not to dismiss the platform but to offer insights on how it could evolve to better meet ethical standards in UX design.

Ethical UX design is about more than just making websites attractive or easy to use. According to Ghanchi (2021), ethical design prioritizes user respect and informed decision- making rather than business-first strategies. It questions the ethical implications behind persuasive design, dark patterns, and user manipulation. Ethical design should empower users by giving them control and clarity in their digital interactions. In this view, designers must move beyond surface-level usability and commit to social responsibility. One of the central concerns in digital design is the presence of "dark patterns," as discussed by Mathur et al. (2019). These are deceptive interface choices that pressure users into actions they might not otherwise take, such as impulsive purchases or unwanted data sharing. The Deceptive Design (2024) database further categorizes these patterns, with common examples being urgency messages and confirmshaming. Zuboff’s (2019) theory of Surveillance Capitalism also becomes relevant in this context. She explains how digital platforms often prioritize profit through data collection and behavioural tracking, often without the user’s full awareness. Ethical UX design, therefore, must consider how platforms handle user data and how transparent they are about their practices. Costanza-Chock (2020) introduces the idea of design justice, a framework that argues for inclusive design created with marginalized communities, not just for them. The motto “nothing about us without us” calls for active participation of underrepresented groups in the design process. This is particularly important in the South African context, where inequality and diversity are key social concerns. Designers like Shapochka (2025) stress that ethical UX should be mindful of the specific challenges users face, such as high data costs, language barriers, and limited digital literacy. These perspectives form the foundation for critically analysing Superbalist’s interface.

Superbalist’s homepage has a simple, modern look with a white background, clear fonts, and eye-catching images. A navigation bar at the top makes it easy to browse sections like Men, Women, Kids, Home, and Sale. Dropdown menus help users find what they need quickly. Big banner ads with phrases like “Shop Now” or “Limited Time Offer” grab attention. The mobile version keeps the same layout and works well on touch screens, making it easy to use on phones. Product pages are easy to understand and include several photos, short descriptions, size guides, and customer reviews. Users can also filter results to narrow down their search. Overall, the design creates a smooth and enjoyable shopping experience. However, there are small design choices that raise ethical concerns. For example, messages like “Only 2 Left!” create pressure to buy fast, using urgency to influence decisions. Also, newsletter boxes are often already checked, which signs users up without clear permission. These small design tricks can quietly shape how people behave on the site. Although the platform seems to be focused on helping users, some features are more about persuading them to act quickly or give away information. This raises questions about whether the design truly puts users first or if it’s more about boosting sales. For this reason, it’s important to look more closely at Superbalist’s user experience and ask whether it supports user choice or subtly takes it away

From a purely usability standpoint, Superbalist delivers a coherent and helpful user experience. The navigation is straightforward, and users can search or filter items with ease. The checkout process is broken into three clear steps: Shipping, Billing, and Payment, which simplifies the path to purchase. The platform also allows guest checkout, which supports user autonomy by not forcing account creation. Payment options are diverse, including EFT and Payflex, which adds to user convenience. However, ethical questions arise when looking at how the site nudges users through the buying process. For instance, the use of phrases like “Only 2 Left!” and “Selling Fast” are examples of urgency-based dark patterns. As noted in the Deceptive Design (2024) database, these techniques manipulate users into making quicker decisions, often bypassing rational consideration.

Transparency, an important ethical principle, is inconsistently applied. While product prices and descriptions are clearly displayed, other crucial information like shipping fees only appears at the final stage of checkout. This tactic limits the user’s ability to make fully informed choices from the outset and introduces elements of surprise that can erode trust. The user registration process is another area of concern. Though signing up is simple and can be done via email or social media accounts, the newsletter subscription box is often pre-checked. This is a form of confirmshaming that pressures users to share personal data by making the opt-out process uncomfortable or unclear. This small but impactful design choice reflects the broader issue of data ethics in digital platforms. While Superbalist may not engage in obvious surveillance capitalism, the use of subtle manipulation aligns with Zuboff’s (2019) concerns about user data being used for profit.

Accessibility is another significant issue. Although the mobile site is generally responsive and includes touch-friendly design, other aspects fall short. Text often lacks contrast and lines are thin. These omissions make it difficult for visually impaired users to interact with the platform. In a country like South Africa, where digital access and literacy are unevenly distributed, these shortcomings are not just technical issues but ethical ones. Moreover, cultural and linguistic inclusivity is limited. The interface is predominantly in English, with minimal representation of other South African languages or cultural references. While local brands are featured, the broader diversity of South African society is not reflected in the site’s design. According to Shapochka (2025), ethical UX should acknowledge the diverse backgrounds and challenges faced by users. Failing to do so means overlooking a significant portion of the population. Even tools that support informed decisions, such as filters and customer reviews, can be ethically unclear. While they do assist in making better choices, their presentation can be inconsistent. Sometimes critical information is buried or not clearly explained, contributing to cognitive overload. This can be especially problematic for users unfamiliar with online shopping, reinforcing digital divides.

Evaluating Superbalist through an ethical UX lens reveals a complex picture. On one hand, the platform offers functional benefits like guest checkout, clear navigation, and helpful shopping tools. On the other hand, it employs manipulative tactics like urgency messaging and pre-selected consent boxes, which compromise user autonomy. Transparency is inconsistent. While product information is visible, cost disclosures and data policies are not immediately accessible. Users often must dig through the site to understand what personal data is collected and how it is used. This lack of clarity does not align with ethical best practices, which emphasize straightforward communication and user control. As Ghanchi (2021) argues, designers must create safe digital environments where users are not only informed but empowered. Inclusivity is perhaps the weakest aspect of Superbalist’s design. Accessibility for disabled users is limited, and cultural representation is narrow. Costanza- Chock’s (2020) design justice framework urges designers to involve marginalized communities directly in the design process. Superbalist appears to design for users rather than with them, missing an opportunity to create a more inclusive platform. In a multilingual, multicultural society like South Africa, ethical design must reflect this diversity. There are also issues related to digital identity. While users can create accounts easily, options to delete accounts or manage personal data are not prominently featured. This asymmetry of control gives the platform more power over user data than the users themselves. Ethical UX should prioritize user agency in managing digital footprints. Dark patterns contribute to cognitive overload, presenting users with too much information or too many options at once. This is particularly harmful to less experienced users, who may struggle to navigate the interface or understand hidden fees. As Mathur et al. (2019) suggest, these strategies are widespread in e-commerce but should be questioned and avoided in ethical design. To become a leader in ethical UX, Superbalist must adopt more inclusive, transparent, and user-centred practices. This includes offering clearer data policies, improving accessibility, and incorporating diverse cultural elements. Ethical UX is not optional; it is essential to building trustworthy and empowering digital platforms. In conclusion, Superbalist.com demonstrates both strengths and weaknesses when evaluated through the lens of ethical UX design. It offers a visually clean layout, mobile compatibility, and a user-friendly checkout process, all of which contribute to a positive shopping experience. However, beneath this surface lie ethically questionable practices, including dark patterns, hidden costs, limited accessibility, and minimal cultural inclusivity. Ethical UX design is not just about convenience or aesthetics. It is about ensuring fairness, autonomy, transparency, and inclusivity for all users. As theorists like Ghanchi (2021), Zuboff (2019), and Costanza-Chock (2020) highlight, digital platforms must be designed with empathy and responsibility, especially in diverse societies like South Africa. If Superbalist aims to serve a broad and varied user base, it must go beyond functional design to embrace ethical principles fully. By improving accessibility, expanding cultural representation, simplifying data policies, and avoiding manipulative tactics, Superbalist can set a new standard for ethical online retail. In doing so, it not only enhances user trust but also contributes to a more just and inclusive digital environment. Ethical UX should not be seen as a luxury but as a core requirement in shaping the future of online interactions in South Africa and beyond.
