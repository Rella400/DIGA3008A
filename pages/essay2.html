Essay 2: Silenced Online: India’s Farmer Protest and the Digital Coloniality of Platform Compliance

June 13, 2025

While many view the internet as an open and neutral space for sharing ideas and information, it is shaped by political, economic, and corporate interests that control what we see, say, and share. This essay explores how these forces intersect by examining India’s censorship of the 2024 farmer protests on X (formerly Twitter). It argues that this case reveals how governments can control online narratives using global platforms, and how those platforms, built in the Global North, are often tools in enforcing digital dominance. The internet is a global system, but one dominated by powerful countries and large tech companies. Geopolitics looks at how nations use their power across borders, including on the internet. Digital coloniality is a modern form of control where Global North platforms shape how the Global South is represented and treated online. In this case, India used local laws to suppress protest content, while X, a U.S. based company, enforced these actions. This highlights the ongoing tension between a country’s right to control its digital space, and the colonial-like structures of digital platforms that are used to do so. The case also raises questions about who controls digital freedom, and how past systems of power are reproduced in today’s internet.

In February 2024, farmers in India launched large-scale protests, marching to Delhi from Punjab and Haryana. They demanded guaranteed minimum prices for their crops and relief from debts. In reaction, the Indian government used Section 69A of the Information Technology Act to order X to block 177 accounts connected to protest organizers, journalists, and activists. The government also threatened legal action against platform staff under the IT Rules 2021. X followed these orders by blocking the accounts within India but left them accessible to users in other countries. It released a public statement expressing concern for freedom of speech and took the issue to the Karnataka High Court. At the same time, authorities cut off internet access in parts of Punjab and Haryana, creating what activists called a "digital curfew." Major media outlets like Reuters, Al Jazeera, and the Financial Times covered the situation. Social media, especially Reddit, showed public anger and frustration. Users criticized X for giving in to government pressure and discussed how this affects digital rights. This case shows how government power, corporate actions, and user experiences are connected in today's online world.

This case can be understood using three key ideas: digital coloniality, geopolitical control of information, and internet geographies. Digital coloniality describes how power from the colonial era is continued through digital systems. Birhane (2020) explains that technologies and platforms from the Global North control how people in the Global South use, create, and appear in digital spaces. These platforms rarely meet local needs and often increase inequality. Geopolitical control of information refers to how governments use laws and policies to shape or limit what is shared online. Ballatore, Graham, and Sen (2017) argue that companies like Google and X don’t just reflect information, they control who sees what, depending on political and economic interests. Their research shows that countries like the U.S. dominate online content. Internet geographies, as discussed by Graham (2014), look at how digital information flows unevenly, usually favouring richer, English-speaking countries. This causes information imbalance, where Global South countries are underrepresented and rely on outside platforms. In the farmer protest case, India used local laws to silence protest voices but relied on a U.S. based platform to do so. This shows how digital coloniality works: postcolonial governments use foreign tech to control their own people. X, acting both as a tech company and as a tool of state control, is a clear example of how online platforms and political power are deeply linked.

The censorship of India’s 2024 farmer protest shows how political power plays out through digital platforms. Using Section 69A and the IT Rules 2021, the Indian government forced X to block content. These laws give the state wide powers to censor without public explanation, creating an unclear environment where protest can easily be silenced. X, a U.S. company, followed the government’s demands to avoid legal consequences. It still filed a legal challenge, but by limiting content in India while keeping it visible elsewhere, it drew digital boundaries. This allowed the government to control the local narrative while appearing open internationally. Ballatore et al. (2017) describe how platforms adjust content visibility based on regional pressures, often in favour of powerful actors. This also demonstrates digital coloniality. India, a postcolonial nation, used Western technology to silence rural voices, much like colonial powers once did. The structure and rules of platforms like X were not made to support grassroots movements in places like India. Instead, they serve state and elite interests. As Birhane (2020) points out, this is algorithmic colonization: digital tools repeating systems of inequality. Farmers, Sikh activists, and local reporters were pushed offline by systems not built with them in mind. Graham (2014) and Ballatore et al. (2017) explain that who gets seen and heard online depends on infrastructure like internet access and publishing networks. India’s use of legal tools became an extra barrier, deciding which voices were allowed. The result: protest stories were hidden from local audiences and lost in the global algorithm. X’s role here shows that platforms are not neutral. They work within a system shaped by politics and economics and often must obey powerful governments to stay active in those regions. So digital coloniality isn’t only imposed by Western countries, it can also be continued by local governments using global tech to maintain control.

News reports and social media both played important roles in how this censorship story was shared. Traditional news outlets like Reuters, Al Jazeera, and the Financial Times gave clear, fact-based coverage. They explained the government’s orders, included statements from X, and shared expert and public reactions. Their reporting framed the issue as a political and legal clash between India and the platform. Social media, especially Reddit and X, showed the emotional and personal side. People reacted in real-time, calling the censorship authoritarian and dangerous. One Reddit user said, "Twitter caves in and suppresses accounts supporting the farmer protest in India." Together, these sources show how information is spread in a layered digital system. Mainstream media shape official stories, while social media give space for public opinion and resistance. But both are limited by the platforms they use, which control what is easy to find and share. In this case, who got visibility depended not only on content but also on how platforms organized and filtered that content. This highlights how control over what we see online is a form of power. That power can be used by governments, corporations, or the platform algorithms themselves. This event shows how digital coloniality is not just about access, but about who gets to speak, be heard, and remembered.

In conclusion, India’s 2024 censorship of the farmer protests on X highlights how the internet is shaped by political and global power. The Indian government used its legal tools to remove critical voices, and X, a global tech company, went along with it. This reveals how digital platforms are caught between state pressure and global expectations. The case shows that digital coloniality is not only about outside control, but also about how local governments use foreign platforms to keep power. When platforms follow national laws that limit speech, they risk helping governments silence marginalized groups. This reinforces inequality in the digital space. As more countries push for digital control and platforms try to balance local rules with global freedom, the internet may become more divided and political. To prevent this, we must look closely at the systems behind digital control, not just the laws and companies, but also the old power structures built into today’s technology.
